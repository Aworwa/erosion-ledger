The Math, Walked Through

The whole dashboard is four arithmetic steps. Values below are the current trailing-twelve-month figures — they update with each quarterly release.

Δ(debt/GDP)  =  (r − g) × d  +  p g = nominal GDP growth · r = avg coupon on the debt · d = debt ratio (the gearing) · p = primary deficit, % of GDP
1

The spread: g − r = − = pts

The debt stock accrues interest at % a year — the blended coupon on all $T outstanding, not today’s market yield; old cheap paper is still in the average. Meanwhile the economy underneath it grows % a year in nominal terms. The economy is outrunning the debt’s own compounding by points annually. This spread is the engine; everything else is gearing.

2

Gear it by the debt ratio: × = pts of GDP per year

The spread is a percentage of the debt, but the scoreboard is measured in GDP — converting between them means multiplying by d. In dollars: a year of interest accrues % × $T ≈ $T of debt growth, while % growth adds ≈ $T of new GDP to the denominator. Run both effects and the existing stock shrinks about points as a share of the economy. That is the erosion: with zero new borrowing, debt/GDP would fall from % to roughly % in a year — without a dollar being repaid.

3

Subtract the new digging: −

The government isn’t at zero new borrowing — it spends about % of GDP (≈$T/yr) more than it taxes before interest. That fresh borrowing converts to ratio points one-for-one: + points a year of accretion. Note what is not in this term: the ≈$T of interest is excluded, because it was already counted in step 1 through r. That is why p is the primary deficit and not the ≈% headline deficit — using the headline would double-count the interest.

4

The net: erosion vs. accretion → drift pts/yr

The machine erodes points; the deficit rebuilds of them; the ratio moves by the difference. (The headline figure in the strip above averages the four quarterly calculations rather than multiplying the rounded strip values through — same answer to a decimal.) And it matches observation: debt/GDP was % at quarter-end and % at — roughly flat while headline deficits ran at a ≈$T-a-year pace. That flatness is the whole point of this ledger.

Method & Sources

The identity. With d = debt/GDP, r = average nominal interest rate on the stock, g = nominal GDP growth, and p = primary deficit (% of GDP): d′ = d × (1+r)/(1+g) + p. The change is approximately (r − g) × d + p. When g exceeds r, the existing stock shrinks relative to the economy; the primary deficit is the new borrowing piled on top. Interest is excluded from p because it already lives inside r — adding the headline deficit would double-count it.

Debt measure. Debt held by the public (Treasury “Debt to the Penny”), not gross debt — intragovernmental holdings are the government owing itself. End-of-quarter level over that quarter's nominal GDP (SAAR).

Nominal GDP. BEA quarterly releases, current-dollar GDP, SAAR percent change. 2026 Q2 = 8.0% (2nd est.), Q1 = 6.1%. Levels chained back from the Q2 2026 level of $32.49T; small differences vs. vintage levels reflect annual revisions.

Average rate. Treasury Fiscal Data, “Average Interest Rates on U.S. Treasury Securities,” total interest-bearing debt, quarter-end month. It moves slowly: the stock reprices only as old paper rolls, which is exactly why issuance strategy matters.

Primary deficit. Headline deficit less net interest, from Treasury Monthly Statements and CRFB tallies: FY24 ≈ $0.95T (3.3% of GDP), FY25 ≈ 2.7%, FY26 running ≈ 3.0–3.1% (10-month deficit $1.8T, gross interest $1.17T). Quarterly values are the fiscal-year rate, an estimate by construction.

Scenario engine. Annual steps from 2026 Q2, iterating the exact identity d′ = d × (1+r)/(1+g) + p with all four terms held where you set them. One deliberate simplification: in reality r is not a lever you set but a stock that reprices slowly — roughly a quarter of the debt rolls over each year at market rates — so a “bond revolt” arrives over several years, not instantly. Holding r flat keeps the levers legible at the cost of that nuance.
